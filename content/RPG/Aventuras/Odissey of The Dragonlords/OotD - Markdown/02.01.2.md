The wine still sings in your veins, a warm and giddy haze that makes the world sway gently on its axis. Your laughter, which seemed so fitting in the tavern, dies in your throats as you are ushered into the royal palace. The transition is jarring. Gone is the warmth of the common room, replaced by the deep, chilling cold of polished marble that seems to drink the sound of your unsteady footsteps.

You are led through cavernous halls lit by flickering torches. The light skitters across immense tapestries that hang from ceiling to floor, their woven legends seeming to writhe in the dancing shadows. With every lurching step, you see scenes of impossible grandeur: Dragonlords locked in combat with multi-headed beasts, silver dragons breathing silent fire upon armies of titans, and the founding of Estoria itself, a city born from war and conquest. The eyes of the woven heroes and monsters seem to follow you, their silent judgment a heavy weight on your shoulders.

Finally, you are brought to a halt before two colossal bronze doors, which swing open without a sound. The throne room is not a room; it is a void. An immense, dark space whose ceiling is lost to shadow, flanked by stone pillars so thick they resemble ancient trees. The air is still and heavy, and it swallows all sound, leaving only the frantic pounding of your own hearts. At the far end, raised on a dais of black basalt, sits a single point of light and authority.

There, upon a throne that seems forged from the weapons of fallen enemies, sits Pythor. He is not just a king; he is an image of war given form. Clad in magnificent bronze armor that drinks the torchlight and glows with a soft, internal heat, he is impossibly still. His powerful frame is relaxed, yet coiled with a palpable tension, like a lion at rest. A formidable beard frames a face that looks as though it were chiseled from granite, and beneath a simple bronze circlet, his eyes are embers burning in the dark. He does not move as you approach, his unflinching gaze cutting through your drunken fog, pinning you in place. When he finally speaks, his voice isn't loud, but it resonates in the stone and in your very bones, a low baritone like the grinding of tectonic plates.

"You have had your fun," the King-God rumbles, a statement, not a question. "Now, you will tell me why you have been summoned by the Oracle."
