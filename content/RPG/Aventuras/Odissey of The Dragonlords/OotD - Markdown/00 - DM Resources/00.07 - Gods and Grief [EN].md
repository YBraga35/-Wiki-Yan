## Gods and Grief  
*A series of character studies*

*Credit to TheEternalW on the Odyssey of the Dragonlords discord as they participated in the conversation that led to writing this piece*

*It is worth noting that these stages of grief are well-known, they aren’t regarded as fact or even a healthy way to handle grief. There are various studies that may be worth researching, if you are interested in the concept and current thought about it in psychological fields of academia, but this document presents the stages as more of a way to examine characters using Tropes than any diagnoses.*

### **Denial** \- *Kyrah*  
Kyrah deals with the ‘death’ of her mother poorly, and is, as the God who spends the most time with the party, the one they are most likely to observe in her patterns of grief. Mytros may have been Kyrah’s sole confidant, as her father has always been somewhat stoic, and she and Vallus have a very strained relationship. 

Kyrah seemingly takes her grief in stride, as she does all matters \- brushing it off with humour and attempting to make light of things, but more so than any of her siblings, Kyrah carries a great deal of weight with her from the First War. She feels responsible for Estor’s atrocities, and that guilt eats at her in a way that doesn’t bother Vallus. She buries herself in stories and busies herself with the affairs of mortals so she doesn’t have to deal with her grief and her regret, rather than processing it. 

Beyond this, Kyrah’s obsession with stories may stem from her desire to make people more than they are \- elevating mere mortals to heroic status, making people immortal such that they will never be forgotten, perhaps because she subconsciously does not want to lose another loved one ever again, and this is her way of making them last forever.

### **Anger** \- *Pythor*  
While Pythor’s comment about missing his mother could be read in a joking manner, it is a shockingly honest view into his mindset. He may actually resent his mother, blaming her for the situation he is in, for allowing things to get to where they are now, for not doing enough, for *leaving them.* 

Certainly he understands that Mytros had to do what she did, but in his quote, he reveals that he may have actually preferred the chance to have fought and lost the First War rather than giving up and surrendering to a peace that could not last. He loved combat, both as a god and before his apotheosis. He may even resent the fact that he is *God of Battle* but is unable to meaningfully fight the Titans in the only way he knows how, and has been forced to watch them use the Oath of Peace to strengthen themselves, and demand sacrifices of him, such as the potential sacrifice of his daughter, Anora. 

It makes sense then, that coupled with his many losses over the centuries (his falling out with his wife, Hexia, the death of Adonis, the loss of his mother, his inability to fight) that he now lashes out in drunken stupors, unable to tell friend from foe at times. 

His anger is palpable. Furious with his mother for causing this set of circumstances, with the Titans, with Hexia for her tormenting of him and the few people to bring happiness into his life,  and ultimately with himself for his inability to do anything about the situation he finds himself in.

### **Depression** \- *Volkan*  
Volkan was always somewhat stoic, never showing his feelings, and always busying himself with his work. After his wife’s death and apotheosis, he appears to have buried himself in his work, becoming more distant to his children, showing affection through gifts and other tokens. 

He meditated on the nature of warfare, and perhaps due to the loss of his wife, hoped to revolutionize warfare by implementing constructs to take the place of mortal suffering. It’s also worth noting that of the Gods, only Volkan’s oathsworn survived the First War. He and Rizon may have had the chance to share in their grief and losses together, but ultimately, she died as well, leaving Volkan with few ‘equals’ with which he could work through his pain, leaving him unable to aid his children, whom were all closer to their mother than they were to him, and with the ‘loss’ of his adoptive daughter Hexia as well, Volkan found himself very alone, hiding in his workshop with his pain, busying himself with the forge rather than dealing with his grief.

### **Bargaining** \- *Vallus*  
Vallus spends each night of her immortal life at the Temple of the Five, praying to her mother, Mytros. In this regard, it appears Vallus has somewhat compartmentalized her grief. She spends her days working, busying herself with the city’s civic duties, but by night, she kneels before the statue of Mytros and prays. 

Vallus lives in her mother’s shadow. She has effectively taken over the role of patron goddess of the city, and has ruled it for most of the city’s existence, but most priests pay lip service to Mytros, most prayers go to the Goddess of Dawn, and Vallus is left to deal with the problems Mytros left behind when she ascended. 

In this interpretation, it makes sense Vallus is working through a lot of complicated emotions, which she only feels able to share with Mytros, praying to her for guidance, seeking aid from her mother in the seemingly impossible task of leading a land that is inevitably going to be thrown back into war. 

### **Acceptance** \- *Narsus*  
Of Mytros’ children, Narsus is the one to have worked through his grief. In this interpretation, I view Narsus as having always abhorred violence and warfare. He didn’t *want* to become Oathsworn of anyone, because that meant he’d have to fight \- something he was bad at, and which he actively did not wish to engage in. When he obtained godhood, he retreated to the city that bore his mother’s name, and focused more on healing the wounded and inspiring the masses, as well as indulging his own vices and finally feeling free of the role he had to play as a dragon. 

Mytros’ death freed him of many obligations, and while his family may have resented him for this, it is entirely possible he had been carrying a great deal of resentment against his family for forcing the role of a warrior on to him when Mytros, as Balmytria, made the agreement with Xander to form the Dragonlords. 

Narsus had centuries in Aresia to work through his complicated feelings towards his family, and like Vallus, he spent a great deal of his time in prayer with his mother. As is evident by his success in communing with her, even after the Oath of Peace expires and Mytros allegedly falls completely silent, Mytros did not resent her son the way the rest of his family did \- or at the very least, throughout the years and through communing with her, Narus and Mytros made peace with one another, such that Narsus received information from Mytros where Vallus, in all of her prayer, never appeared to get any answers from her mother. 

It is possible then that we can interpret Narsus’ success as something he was able to achieve only because he had managed to go through the stages of grief and come to a place of acceptance of the relationship he and his mother had in her life, and her death.
